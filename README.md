## AI Economics is Energy Economics (2026)

I began this project expecting primarily to study the economics of AI infrastructure. Instead, every question led me back to energy. Where would the power come from? How quickly could a project connect to the grid? What would happen to the investment while it waited?

I built a bottom-up financial model of a 100 MW AI data center across Norway, Saudi Arabia, India, the United States, and Ireland. The model compares contracted and merchant structures under bear, base, and bull scenarios. It includes country-level power and capital costs, operating cash flows, sensitivities, sourced assumptions, and automated checks.

The result changed how I understood the project: time, not power, is the binding variable in value creation for data center projects. A long grid queue creates holding costs while the market price of compute erodes. Offtake contracts materially improves the economics, but it cannot compensate for entering the market too late.

This is an indicative comparison, not an investible recommendation. It shows which assumptions change the decision and why.

## benchmarkai

I built benchmarkai after spending a month in Singapore asking companies how AI was changing their work. Almost everyone was experimenting with AI, but few could explain how those experiments connected to strategy or measurable business value.

The tool evaluates publicly visible corporate AI activity across three areas:

1. Agentic integration
2. Workforce transformation
3. Quantified business impact

It combines strategic benchmarks with current public signals, confidence ratings, visual comparisons, and cited evidence. The report gives the user a structured way to question what a company has actually disclosed.

Building the tool taught me that the loudest AI signal is not necessarily the most meaningful. Using generative AI to build quickly is easy; building something consistent, testable, and trustworthy takes much longer.

## Injuries in Routesetting

Before business school, I spent years working in rock climbing. Routesetting was developing quickly as a profession, but its safety practices were not keeping pace.

For my undergraduate honors thesis, I conducted international research by surveying 178 routesetters. I compared the results with research on occupational health, climbing injuries, and construction safety. I wanted to understand why routesetters were getting injured and what the industry could do about it.

Sixty-four percent reported a routesetting-related injury. The leading sites were the fingers, shoulders, back, wrists, elbows, and eyes. Common reported causes included overwork, heavy loads, and forerunning. More than 80% reporting an eye injury were not wearing protective eyewear when it happened.

This was my first published research project on a problem I had seen up close. It paved the way for me to tackle more ambitious problems and the better implement solutions.
